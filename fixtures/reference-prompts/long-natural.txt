I am planning a week-long walking trip along a river valley in early autumn and would like help thinking it through. The route is about one hundred and ten kilometres from the reservoir at the head of the valley down to the old harbour town at the estuary. There are villages roughly every fifteen kilometres, but two of the stretches cross open moorland with no shelter, no shops and unreliable phone signal. I will be walking with my sister, who has a weak ankle from an old climbing injury, and with a friend who has never done a multi-day walk before. We can carry day packs but would prefer not to camp, so we need a bed each night. The forecast for that part of the country at that time of year is usually changeable: bright mornings, wind in the afternoon and a real chance of two wet days in seven. We have a modest budget, we do not want to book a guided tour, and we would like at least one short day in the middle so that people can rest, wash clothes and look around a town. One of us is vegetarian, and all of us would rather eat in a small pub or a cafe than cook. We will arrive by train at the upper end and leave by train from the harbour town, and the only fixed date is the return ticket on the eighth day. Please propose a day-by-day plan with distances, explain how you would handle the two exposed moorland stretches safely, say what each of us should carry, and tell me which decisions I should make now and which can wait until we see the weather. If some part of my plan seems unrealistic, say so plainly and suggest what you would change. Finally, list three questions I should ask the places we stay before I pay a deposit.


Here is everything else I know, in no particular order, so that you can tell me what matters and what does not.

The walk starts on a Saturday. The first bus to the reservoir leaves the market square at ten past seven and takes fifty minutes, and there is only one more that day, just after noon. If we miss the early one we lose half the first day, and the first stretch is one of the two that cross the moor. It is nineteen kilometres with about four hundred metres of climbing in the first third, then a long level section over peat, then a steep descent on a stony track into the first village. My friend walks comfortably at about four kilometres an hour on a pavement and has no idea what her pace is on rough ground. My sister can keep up with anyone on the flat and going uphill, but descents are where her ankle complains, and a stony descent at the end of a long day is exactly the wrong thing. She has a brace and two poles and is stubborn about using neither until it is too late.

The inn in the first village has two rooms and serves food until eight. The next two villages each have a pub with rooms and a small shop that shuts at five and does not open on Sunday. After that there is a market town with a pharmacy, a railway station on a branch line, an outdoor shop and a choice of places to stay. The second moorland stretch comes on what would be the fifth day if we keep to fifteen kilometres a day: twenty-two kilometres, no road crossing for the middle fourteen, one stone shelter with a roof and three walls at roughly the halfway point, and a ford that the guidebook says is ankle deep in summer and can be thigh deep after two days of rain. There is a footbridge four kilometres upstream of the ford, which adds about an hour and a half.

We have eight days of leave in total, including travel home from the harbour town, which takes most of a day by train with two changes. None of us wants to camp. All of us would rather arrive early and sit in a pub garden than arrive in the dark. My sister would like one day with no walking at all, and has suggested the market town. My friend has said, more than once, that she does not want to be the reason anyone turns back, which I take to mean she will not tell us when she is struggling.

Weather in the valley in early autumn is usually settled in the first half of the month and less so later. Mornings can be foggy on the moor until ten or eleven. Sunset moves from about half past seven at the start of the month to about half past six at the end. We can go in either the second or the fourth week.

Kit we already own: waterproof jackets and trousers for all three, two pairs of poles, one paper map of the upper valley only, a first aid kit that was last checked three years ago, one head torch, and a small stove that nobody wants to carry. My sister and I have boots that are well worn in. My friend bought hers last month and has worn them twice, around town.

There is a luggage transfer service that moves bags between the villages on weekdays for a fee per bag per stage, but it does not serve the inn at the end of the first moorland stretch or the farmhouse at the end of the second, because the van cannot get up either lane.

What I would like from you, please, is the following. First, tell me which week you would choose and why, in two or three sentences. Second, lay out a day by day plan with distances, where we sleep, and where the rest day goes, and say plainly which days you think are the risky ones. Third, for each of the two moorland days, give me a turn-back rule that is simple enough to agree on over breakfast and hard to argue with at two in the afternoon, including what we do about the ford. Fourth, tell me what to do about my friend's boots and about the fact that she will not speak up, without making her feel singled out. Fifth, list what is missing from our kit and what we should leave behind, given the luggage service's gaps. Keep the whole answer practical and specific to this route rather than general advice about walking, and if you think the plan is too ambitious for this group, say so and tell me what you would cut.
